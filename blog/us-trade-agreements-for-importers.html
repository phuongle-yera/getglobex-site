US trade agreements that can lower your import duties

By GetGlobex team (getglobex.com) · Published 5 Nov 2026

If you import into the US, where your supplier is located can change the duty you pay. This guide lists the agreements in force today, in plain words, with links to the official pages.

What a trade agreement does for an importer

  • Qualifying goods can enter at reduced or zero normal US duty.
  • "Qualifying" means the product meets the agreement's rules of origin, and you claim the preference when the goods enter the US.
  • Newer US tariffs may still apply on top, so an agreement doesn't always mean zero cost.

Free trade agreements in force

The US has 14 free trade agreements in force, covering 20 countries. Source: USTR.

Note: The US also has a limited trade agreement with Japan, in force since 1 Jan 2020. It lowers or removes US duty on a limited list of goods and is not a full free trade agreement. See CBP: Japan Trade Agreement.

Duty-preference programs

In force:

  • AGOA, the African Growth and Opportunity Act (eligible sub-Saharan African countries, through 31 Dec 2028). Extension: Public Law 119-103, Sec. 2008.
  • Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBERA and CBTPA; CBTPA runs through 30 Sep 2030). See CBP: CBTPA and trade.gov: CBTPA.
  • Haiti HOPE/HELP (apparel, within quotas, through 2028). Extension: Public Law 119-103, Sec. 2009.

Not available now:

  • GSP, the Generalized System of Preferences (lapsed 31 Dec 2020, not renewed). See CBP: GSP.
  • Nepal Trade Preference Program (expired 31 Dec 2025). See trade.gov: Nepal program.

What changed in 2025–26

The US announced many new trade deals in 2025 and 2026. As of 29 Oct 2026 most are frameworks or signed agreements that haven't entered into force yet, and US tariff rules have changed several times. Check the current terms before you plan around them.

How to check before you buy

  1. Find your product's HS code (hts.usitc.gov).
  2. Ask your supplier whether the goods meet the agreement's rules of origin and can provide the paperwork.
  3. Confirm with CBP or a licensed customs broker.
